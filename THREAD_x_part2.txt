Follow-up to this, two days later.

Before running it on a second model I went back and tried to break it.

I found a flaw in my own question wording, re-ran it clean, and one of my two results did not survive.

Here is what happened.
---
17
The claim was "same number, only the scope changes."

Not true. I checked the four questions word by word.

The steps pair also switched from an AVERAGE per person to a TOTAL for everyone. Both pairs changed the example factors I suggested.

Two variables moving, not one.
---
18
So I rewrote them as strict minimal pairs. Same stem, same suggested factors, only the thing being asked about narrows. Then re-ran everything: 260 fresh answers.

The bridge result held up.

19/20 copied when wide. 1/20 when narrow. p = 5.8e-9.
---
19
The steps result collapsed.

20/20 copied when wide.
20/20 copied when narrow.

Zero effect. I had predicted 5 or fewer, written down before the run.
---
20
Why it collapsed isn't what I first assumed.

I checked the model's own answers to each question. Narrowing the question moved its typical answer 201x. Switching average to total moved it only 31x.

Scope did MORE work, not less. My first explanation was backwards.
---
21
The real problem: I attached 1,100,000, and that number was reachable from BOTH halves of my new pair.

The narrow question's own typical answer is 30,600. So 1.1M is only 36x away. Easily justified.

I needed something above ~1,760,000 to force a refusal. I missed by 1.6x.
---
22
So the steps test didn't refute anything. It just failed to test.

Worth being precise about, because "my experiment failed" and "my hypothesis is wrong" are different claims and it's tempting to report the braver one.
---
23
The embarrassing bit: I'd written down this exact failure mode days earlier.

"A designed experiment picks the numeral to sit at a target distance, rather than inheriting it."

Then I inherited it. The baselines didn't exist till mid-run, and I never stopped to look.
---
24
What survived got stronger.

The rule is not about scope. It's distance from the model's own answers, measured in that question's own spread.

Fit that rule on the 16 old cells. Predict the 4 new ones, from questions that didn't exist yet.

4 out of 4.
---
25
Also checked whether "spread" was real or just sampling noise.

Re-measured every baseline at temperature 0.7. The ordering of questions by spread holds, rank correlation 0.90.

So it's a property of the question, not of the sampler.
---
26
Honest limits, now that I've looked harder:

One clean causal pair, not two. One model. 20 answers a cell.

Nine questions is really three domains. The cross-domain number is 0.83, not the 0.86 I quoted.

The threshold sits in a gap where I measured nothing.
